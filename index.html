📚 Complete Guide to "SFT vs. LoRA vs. ICL" Paper

Your crash course to confidently discuss arXiv:2511.00130 with Sherry Moore

Paper: "A Comparative Analysis of LLM Adaptation: SFT, LoRA, and ICL in Data-Scarce Scenarios"
Authors: Bernd Bohnet, Rumen Dangovski, Kevin Swersky, Sherry Moore, Arslan Chaudhry, Kathleen Kenealy, Noah Fiedel
Affiliation: Google DeepMind | Date: Oct 2025 | Submitted to: ICLR 2026
Base model used: Gemma 3 (4B)

PART 1: THE FUNDAMENTALS (Everything you need before the paper makes sense)

Before diving into the paper, let's make sure you understand every concept it relies on. If you already know some of these, skim them — but don't skip, because I'll connect each concept back to the paper's arguments.

1.1 What is "LLM Adaptation"?

A pre-trained LLM (like Gemma 3, Llama, GPT) is a generalist — it's been trained on massive amounts of internet text to predict the next token. But when you want to use it for a specific task (say, tagging parts of speech, or answering medical questions), the model needs to be adapted. Adaptation means modifying the model so it becomes better at your specific task.

There are three main approaches this paper compares:

  1. SFT (Supervised Fine-Tuning) — You update ALL the model's weights using labeled examples. It's the most direct approach but also the most aggressive.
  2. LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) — You freeze the original weights and add tiny "adapter" matrices that learn the task. Much cheaper, much less disruptive.
  3. ICL (In-Context Learning) — You don't update any weights at all. You just put examples in the prompt and the model learns from them at inference time.

The key tension this paper explores: each method has a different trade-off between learning the new task and remembering what the model already knew.

1.2 Catastrophic Forgetting — The Central Villain

Catastrophic forgetting is when a neural network learns something new and forgets what it previously knew. It's been studied since 1989 (McCloskey & Cohen).

Intuition: Imagine you're a skilled chef who can cook 100 dishes. Someone sends you to a 3-day intensive course on making perfect sushi. You come back making incredible sushi — but you've forgotten how to cook 80 of your other dishes. That's catastrophic forgetting.

Why does it happen? Neural networks store knowledge in their weights (the numbers that define the model). When you fine-tune on new data, you're overwriting those weights. If you update them too aggressively (high learning rate, many steps), the old knowledge gets erased.

Why is it called "catastrophic"? Because it's not gradual. The model doesn't slowly degrade — it can go from perfect to zero on a task in just a few training steps. The paper shows NQ (Natural Questions) accuracy dropping to literally 0% after just a few steps of SFT on a tagging task.

This is the #1 problem the paper is trying to understand: Which adaptation method causes the most forgetting? Which preserves the most knowledge? And why?

1.3 Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) — The Aggressive Approach

What it is: You take the pre-trained model and continue training it on your task's labeled data. You update ALL the parameters (every weight in every layer).

How it works:
1. Start with pre-trained weights W (the model already knows language)
2. Feed in your task data (e.g., sentences with POS tags)
3. Compute loss (how wrong the model's predictions are)
4. Backpropagate gradients through ALL layers
5. Update ALL weights: W_new = W_old - learning_rate × gradient

Pros:
- Fastest learning — can pick up a new skill with as few as 16 examples
- Highest peak performance on the target task

Cons:
- Catastrophic forgetting — because you're modifying ALL weights, old knowledge gets overwritten
- Computationally expensive (updating billions of parameters)

In this paper's experiments:
- SFT uses learning rates between 10⁻³ and 10⁻⁴
- Batch size of 8
- The paper shows SFT causes NQ accuracy to crash to 0 within a few training steps
- Even lowering the learning rate doesn't fully fix it — lower LR prevents learning the task, but forgetting still happens

1.4 LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) — The Surgical Approach

This is the most important concept to understand for this paper. Let me explain it thoroughly.

The Core Idea

Instead of updating the full weight matrix W (which might be 4096 × 4096 = 16.8M parameters per layer), LoRA freezes W and adds a small low-rank update:

W_adapted = W_frozen + ΔW

where ΔW = A × B

A: shape (d, r)    — small matrix
B: shape (r, d)    — small matrix
r: rank            — much smaller than d (e.g., r=4 instead of d=4096)

Example with numbers:
- Original weight matrix W: 4096 × 4096 = 16,777,216 parameters
- LoRA with rank r=4:
- A: 4096 × 4 = 16,384 parameters
- B: 4 × 4096 = 16,384 parameters
- Total: 32,768 parameters (0.2% of original!)

So you're training 0.2% of the parameters instead of 100%.

Why Low-Rank Works

The key insight (from the original LoRA paper, Hu et al. 2022) is that task-specific adaptations are inherently low-rank — meaning you don't need to change all 16.8M numbers to learn a new skill. A small number of directions in weight space is sufficient.

Analogy: Think of the model as a vast music studio with thousands of knobs. SFT turns ALL the knobs. LoRA only turns a few carefully chosen knobs. The few knobs are enough to change the "sound" (output) for your specific task, but leave the rest of the studio untouched.

LoRA's Hyperparameters in This Paper

  • Rank (r): Controls how many parameters are trainable. Paper uses r=1, 4, 8, 16, 32. Default is r=4.
  • Higher rank = more expressive capacity = faster learning, but more potential for forgetting
  • Lower rank = more constrained = slower learning, but less forgetting
  • Learning rate: 0.005 (higher than SFT because fewer parameters need bigger updates)
  • Batch size: 8

The "LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less" View

A prior influential paper (Biderman et al. 2024, "LoRA learns less and forgets less") argued that LoRA's constrained nature inherently prevents forgetting. By restricting updates to a low-rank subspace, LoRA acts as a regularizer — it physically cannot make the drastic weight shifts that cause knowledge degradation in SFT.

The "Illusion of Equivalence" Finding

Another prior paper (Shuttleworth et al. 2024) found something subtle: even when LoRA and SFT achieve identical performance on a target task, they learn structurally different solutions:
- SFT makes small, diffuse changes across ALL weights
- LoRA introduces "intruder dimensions" — new high-ranking singular vectors that are orthogonal to the pre-trained weights

These intruder dimensions are learned solely from the fine-tuning data and can be brittle. So LoRA isn't just "SFT but smaller" — it's fundamentally different in what it learns.

This paper builds on these prior findings and adds a new dimension: WHERE in the model the changes happen (the weight delta analysis).

1.5 In-Context Learning (ICL) — The No-Update Approach

What it is: You don't update any weights. You simply put examples in the model's prompt/context, and the model uses them to figure out the task at inference time.

Example prompt for POS tagging:

Task: Tag each word with its part of speech.

Sentence: The cat sat on the mat
Tags:    DET NOUN VERB ADP DET NOUN

Sentence: She quickly ran to the store
Tags:    PRON ADV VERB ADP DET NOUN

Sentence: The dog barked loudly
Tags:    ???

The model looks at the examples in the prompt and predicts the tags. No gradient updates, no weight changes.

Pros:
- Zero catastrophic forgetting (you never touch the weights!)
- Instant — no training needed
- Flexible — change examples, change behavior

Cons:
- Limited by context window (you can only fit so many examples)
- For complex skills (like planning), few examples in context aren't enough — the model simply can't learn
- Performance on smaller models is often inadequate for hard tasks

In this paper:
- ICL uses up to 32K tokens of context (with Gemma 3)
- 1 to 128 examples in context
- The paper finds ICL is great for knowledge tasks but bad for complex skills like planning

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting

A variant of ICL where examples include intermediate reasoning steps. E.g., instead of just showing question→answer, you show question→reasoning→answer. This helps on complex tasks but the paper doesn't focus on CoT — it uses standard ICL.

Many-Shot ICL

Recent work (Agarwal et al. 2024) showed that with very long context windows (1M+ tokens), you can put thousands of examples in context — "many-shot ICL" — which can rival SFT on some tasks. But this paper focuses on the "few-shot" regime (up to 128 examples), not many-shot.

1.6 Skill vs. Knowledge — The Paper's Key Distinction

This is a crucial conceptual framework the paper introduces. The paper divides tasks into two categories:

Skill-Based Tasks

Tasks that require the model to acquire a new capability — a new way of processing input.

Examples from the paper:
- UPOS (Universal POS Tagging): Label each word with one of 17 universal grammatical categories (noun, verb, adjective, etc.)
- XPOS (Language-Specific POS Tagging): Fine-grained POS tags with morphological details (tense, case, etc.)
- FEATS (Morphological Feature Prediction): Assign detailed grammatical attributes (tense, number, gender)
- LEMMA (Lemmatization): Reduce inflected words to base form ("running" → "run", "better" → "good")
- Head (Syntactic Head Prediction): For each word, identify its governing word in the dependency tree
- ANLI (Adversarial Natural Language Inference): Determine if one sentence entails/contradicts/neutral another — specifically designed to fool models
- Blocksworld: A planning task — rearrange blocks into a goal configuration (PDDL formulation)
- Logistics: A planning task — transport packages by coordinating trucks and airplanes
- WSC (Winograd Schema Challenge): Coreference resolution requiring common-sense reasoning

Key property of skills: The model might have seen similar tasks in pre-training, but they weren't the primary training objective. The model needs to learn how to do the task, not just recall facts.

Knowledge-Based Tasks

Tasks that primarily test whether the model knows specific information.

Examples from the paper:
- NQ (Natural Questions): Real Google Search queries — model must generate answers from its internal knowledge (no context provided). Used as the reference task to measure forgetting.
- BoolQ: Yes/no reading comprehension questions
- GPQA: Graduate-level, "Google-proof" multiple-choice questions
- GSM8K: Grade-school math word problems

Key property of knowledge tasks: The model either knows the answer or it doesn't. You can't "learn" to know a fact through practice — either the model has the knowledge or it doesn't.

Why this distinction matters:
- Skills can be learned through SFT and LoRA (they change how the model processes input)
- Knowledge can be injected through ICL (just put the fact in context) but is hard to instill through fine-tuning (and fine-tuning risks erasing existing knowledge)
- The paper's central finding: The best adaptation method depends on whether you're teaching a skill or injecting knowledge

1.7 Weight Deltas (ΔW) — The Paper's Key Analytical Tool

This is the most technically interesting part of the paper, so let me explain it carefully.

What is ΔW?

ΔW (weight delta) = the difference between the fine-tuned model's weights and the original pre-trained model's weights:

ΔW = W_finetuned - W_original

For SFT: ΔW is the full update to all weights (large, diffuse changes)
For LoRA: ΔW = A × B (the low-rank update, small, concentrated changes)

Why Analyze ΔW?

By looking at WHERE in the model the changes happen (which layers, which modules), you can understand WHY different methods behave differently:
- If changes are concentrated in upper layers → the method is modifying high-level decision-making
- If changes are spread across all layers → the method is modifying everything (including low-level features that other tasks depend on → forgetting)
- If changes are small in magnitude → less disruption to existing knowledge
- If changes are large → more disruption

How the Paper Visualizes ΔW

They create heatmaps (Figure 12 for LoRA, Figure 13 for SFT):
- Y-axis: Model layers (0 to ~31, from bottom to top of the transformer)
- X-axis: Number of training samples (32, 64, 128, 256)
- Color: Magnitude of weight updates (dark blue = small, green/yellow = large)
- Separate heatmaps for attention weights and MLP weights
- Multiple heatmaps at different training steps (800, 1600, 3000)

What They Found (Preview — detailed in Part 2)

LoRA:
- Changes are heavily concentrated in upper layers (layers 20-31)
- Lower layers (0-8) are essentially untouched
- The pattern is established very early (by step 800) and stays stable
- This explains why LoRA preserves general knowledge — it doesn't touch the low-level features that other tasks depend on

SFT:
- Changes are >10x larger in magnitude than LoRA
- Changes are more spread across layers
- This explains the severe forgetting — you're rewriting everything

1.8 The Base Model: Gemma 3 (4B)

The paper uses Gemma 3 (4B) — Google's open-weights model with 4 billion parameters. Key properties relevant to the paper:

  • Architecture: Decoder-only transformer (LLaMA-style)
  • Layers: ~31 layers (inferred from the ΔW analysis showing layers 0-31)
  • Normalization: RMSNorm (relevant — the paper mentions RMSNorm attenuates gradients in early blocks)
  • Residual connections: Present (might mitigate gradient vanishing)
  • Context window: Supports 32K+ tokens (important for ICL experiments)

The choice of a single 4B model is deliberate — the paper isn't trying to be comprehensive across model sizes. It's a controlled study. The authors acknowledge this limitation.

1.9 Key Concepts from Prior Work (Referenced in the Paper)

"LoRA learns less and forgets less" (Biderman et al. 2024)

  • Established that LoRA occupies a different point on the learning-forgetting Pareto curve than SFT
  • LoRA: lower target-task performance, higher knowledge retention
  • SFT: higher target-task performance, lower knowledge retention

"Illusion of Equivalence" (Shuttleworth et al. 2024)

  • Even when LoRA and SFT reach identical target-task accuracy, they learn structurally different solutions
  • SFT: small, diffuse changes across all weights
  • LoRA: introduces "intruder dimensions" — new high-ranking singular vectors orthogonal to pre-trained weights
  • These intruder dimensions are brittle and can cause forgetting too (LoRA isn't immune)

"BERT rediscovers the classical NLP pipeline" (Tenney et al. 2019)

  • Found that high-level, task-specific decision rules live in late layers of the model
  • This is the theoretical basis for why LoRA's concentration in upper layers makes sense — it's updating where the decisions are made

Many-Shot ICL (Agarwal et al. 2024)

  • With very long context, thousands of examples in context can rival SFT
  • Also introduced "Reinforced ICL" using self-generated rationales
  • This paper deliberately uses fewer shots and no Reinforced ICL — focusing on the data-scarce regime

PART 2: THE PAPER IN DEPTH — Section by Section

2.1 Introduction — The Problem Statement

The core question: When you need to adapt an LLM for a specific task, and you don't have much data, which method should you use — SFT, LoRA, or ICL?

Why this matters:
- Full fine-tuning is expensive and causes forgetting
- LoRA is cheaper but has its own trade-offs
- ICL is free but limited
- No one had done a systematic, controlled comparison in the data-scarce regime

The paper's four contributions:
1. Systematic comparison of SFT, LoRA, and ICL in low-data regimes
2. Assessment of catastrophic forgetting from iterative training on small datasets
3. Analysis of the trade-off between task accuracy and forgetting as a function of hyperparameters (learning rate, LoRA rank)
4. Weight delta (ΔW) analysis revealing that LoRA's stability comes from modifications to high-level layers established early in training

Important methodological choice: They deliberately use standard/default settings from common frameworks — no dropout, no early stopping, no hyperparameter optimization. The goal isn't SOTA performance; it's understanding fundamental trade-offs.

2.2 Experimental Setup

Model

  • Gemma 3 (4B) — single model for controlled comparison

Data Sizes

  • Training examples scale logarithmically: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024, 2048, 4096, 8192
  • This "data-scarce" regime is what makes ICL comparable to fine-tuning (you can't put 8192 examples in context, but you can put 128)

Training Settings

  • Batch size: 8 (small, to accommodate small training sets)
  • LoRA learning rate: 0.005
  • SFT learning rate: 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁴ (they experiment with different rates)
  • LoRA rank: default 4, but also experiment with 1, 8, 16, 32
  • Framework: Kauldron (Google's framework for Gemma models)
  • ICL context: up to 32K tokens

Tasks

Skill-based (need to learn a new capability):
- UPOS, XPOS, FEATS, LEMMA, Head (linguistic/NLP tasks)
- ANLI (natural language inference)
- Blocksworld, Logistics (planning)
- WSC (coreference resolution)

Knowledge-based (test existing knowledge):
- NQ, BoolQ, GPQA, GSM8K

Measuring Forgetting

  • Reference task: NQ (Natural Questions)
  • Before fine-tuning: measure baseline NQ accuracy
  • After fine-tuning: measure NQ accuracy again
  • If NQ accuracy drops → catastrophic forgetting has occurred
  • They also check "instruction following" — does the model still follow instructions for other tasks, or has it become obsessed with the fine-tuning task?

2.3 Results: In-Context Learning (ICL)

What they found:

Skill tasks — ICL is task-dependent:
- UPOS (POS tagging): ICL works well. Accuracy goes from 83.5% (1 example) to 89.4% (128 examples). The model effectively picks up this skill from context.
- Head (syntactic head prediction): Much harder. Accuracy is low but increases slightly to 9.6% with more examples. The model struggles.
- ANLI (adversarial NLI): Accuracy doesn't improve with more examples — even drops. The model can't learn this from context alone.

Knowledge tasks — ICL shows only moderate, non-significant improvements:
- NQ: slight positive trend (model adapting to answer format, not learning new facts)
- GSM8K: slight positive trend
- GPQA: actually decreases after 16 samples

Key takeaway for ICL:

  • ICL is great for simple skills that the model almost knows already (like POS tagging)
  • ICL fails on complex skills (like planning, adversarial reasoning)
  • ICL shows minimal learning on knowledge tasks — you can't teach a model new facts by putting them in context (the model adapts to format, not content)
  • ICL never causes forgetting (obviously — no weights are changed)

2.4 Results: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

What they found (Figure 5):

SFT rapidly learns the skill but catastrophically forgets:
- Even with 16-256 training samples, the model quickly reaches high accuracy on the target task (UPOS)
- But within a few training steps, NQ accuracy drops to 0
- The model's instruction-following capacity is destroyed — it starts trying to POS-tag every input, even instructions for other tasks

Learning rate matters but doesn't solve the problem:
- LR = 10⁻³ (high): Fast learning, immediate catastrophic forgetting
- LR = 5×10⁻⁴ (medium): Learning happens, but forgetting is still severe
- LR = 10⁻⁴ (low): Forgetting is reduced, but the model also fails to learn the task effectively

The fundamental SFT dilemma: You can't have both. High enough LR to learn → forgetting. Low enough LR to preserve knowledge → no learning.

Figure 6 — Head prediction task:
- Training accuracy reaches 100% quickly (even with few samples) — the model memorizes the training data
- But test accuracy remains near-zero with 16 samples and only reaches ~20% with 1024 samples
- This shows SFT overfits severely in low-data regimes — it memorizes rather than generalizes

Key takeaway for SFT:

  • SFT is the fastest learner — can acquire skills with as few as 16 examples
  • But SFT causes the most severe forgetting — NQ goes to zero within steps
  • Lowering learning rate doesn't fix it — it just prevents learning while still causing some forgetting
  • SFT also overfits in low-data regimes (100% train accuracy, near-zero test accuracy)

2.5 Results: LoRA

What they found:

LoRA needs more data than SFT to learn (Figure 8):
- 16 samples: insufficient — LoRA can't learn the task
- 64+ samples: substantial improvement — LoRA starts working
- SFT, in contrast, can learn with just 16 samples

LoRA preserves knowledge better than SFT (Figure 2):
- While learning UPOS effectively, NQ accuracy stays stable (unlike SFT where it crashes to 0)
- This is the key advantage: LoRA learns the skill AND remembers what it knew

But LoRA isn't immune to forgetting (Figure 10):
- With 512+ training samples, NQ accuracy starts declining
- At 8192 samples and 6000 steps, the validation accuracy peaks but NQ is substantially lower
- "LoRA forgets too when more training samples are provided"
- Forgetting starts around 400 training steps with large datasets

LoRA rank matters (Figure 7, Figure 9):
- Higher rank (r=16, r=32) → faster convergence (leftward shift in learning curves)
- Rank 4 and 16: reach peak accuracy (~82.5-83.3%) by step 800
- Rank 1: reaches 83.4% but needs 3000 steps (much slower)
- Diminishing returns for r > 8 with 128 samples
- Both sufficient capacity (rank) AND sufficient data are necessary

Instructions at test time help (Figure 11):
- When the model is trained with few samples (e.g., 8), providing task instructions at test time significantly improves performance
- With more training data, the model internalizes the task and instructions matter less
- But after extensive training (6000+ steps), instruction-following degrades — "a form of forgetting even with LoRA"

Key takeaway for LoRA:

  • LoRA is the balanced approach — learns skills while preserving knowledge
  • But needs a critical mass of data (fails with <32 samples, works from ~64+)
  • LoRA CAN forget with too much training (large datasets + many steps)
  • Higher rank = faster learning but more capacity for disruption
  • The sweet spot: moderate data (64-256 samples) + moderate rank (4-8) + not too many steps

2.6 The Weight Delta (ΔW) Analysis — The Paper's Crown Jewel

This is the most novel and interesting contribution. Let me break it down carefully.

What They Did

They compared the fine-tuned model's weights to the original pre-trained model's weights, layer by layer, to see WHERE the changes happen. They created heatmaps for:
- LoRA (Figure 12) and SFT (Figure 13)
- Attention weights (top row) and MLP weights (bottom row)
- At different training steps: 800, 1600, 3000
- For different training set sizes: 32, 64, 128, 256 samples

Finding 1: LoRA Updates are Highly Layer-Specific

The changes are NOT uniform across the model. They're heavily concentrated in upper layers (layers 20-31).

  • Layers 20-31 (upper): High-magnitude updates (green to yellow on heatmap) — this is where the action is
  • Layers 0-8 (lower): Extremely small updates (dark blue/purple) — essentially untouched
  • Isolated hotspots: A band of higher activity around layer 13 and another around layer 24

Why does this happen? The paper offers a hypothesis:
1. High-level decision rules live in late layers (Tenney et al. 2019 — "BERT rediscovers the classical NLP pipeline"). So updating late layers directly affects the model's task-specific decisions.
2. Cross-entropy loss gradients diminish with depth during backpropagation. Early layers get smaller gradients.
3. RMSNorm further attenuates gradients in early blocks.
4. Residual connections might mitigate this in Gemma (but the effect still holds).
5. Early layers encode reusable features (lexical, orthographic) that are already sufficient for the task — changing them offers little gain and high risk.
6. Attention vs. MLP: Attention layers adjust routing/aggregation (where information flows), while MLPs adjust feature re-weighting (what information means). The majority of changes in both LoRA and SFT occur in attention weights.

Why this explains LoRA's stability: Since LoRA only modifies upper layers (high-level decisions) and leaves lower layers (foundational features) untouched, the model's general capabilities — which depend on those lower layers — are preserved. It's like modifying the top of a building without touching the foundation.

Finding 2: Rapid Convergence of the Update Pattern

The pattern of WHICH layers are updated is established by step 800 and remains remarkably stable through step 3000.

  • The hotspots at layers 13, 24, and 28-31 are already visible at step 800
  • By steps 1600 and 3000, the pattern doesn't fundamentally change
  • Some magnitudes intensify slightly (layers 20-27 get a bit greener), but the distribution of where changes happen is fixed

Why this matters: It means LoRA finds its "adaptation direction" very quickly and sticks with it. The model doesn't gradually spread changes to new layers — it commits early to a specific set of layers and only intensifies within those. This is why early stopping might not help much — the pattern is already set.

Finding 3: Module-Specific Updates

Within the active layers, updates are also not uniform across the training-data axis:
- The hotspot at layer 13 is almost exclusively in the first column (32 samples)
- At layer 24, the largest updates are in the first two columns (32 and 64 samples)

This suggests that different layers become active depending on how much data you have — with very few examples, only the most "sensitive" layers respond.

Finding 4: SFT Changes are 10x Larger

For comparison (Figure 13):
- SFT's weight changes are more than an order of magnitude larger than LoRA's
- The LoRA heatmap scale maxes out at ~0.3 for the L1-Norm
- SFT's changes are 10x+ that scale
- Similar to LoRA, the majority of SFT changes also occur in attention weights (not MLP)
- But unlike LoRA, SFT's changes are more spread across layers — it touches everything, just with varying intensity

Why this explains SFT's forgetting: SFT makes massive, diffuse changes across all layers. Even if upper layers get the most changes, the changes to lower layers are still large enough to overwrite the foundational features that other tasks depend on. It's like renovating every floor of a building — the foundation gets damaged even if the top floors get the most work.

2.7 Conclusions — The Practical Framework

The paper provides a clear decision framework:

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│           WHICH ADAPTATION METHOD TO USE?                │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                         │
│  Is the task a SKILL (new capability)?                  │
│  ├─ YES, and data is extremely limited (<32 examples)   │
│  │  → SFT (but use caution — expect forgetting)         │
│  ├─ YES, and data is moderate (64+ examples)            │
│  │  → LoRA (best balance of learning + retention)       │
│  └─ YES, and the skill is simple (model almost knows it) │
│     → Try ICL first (free, no forgetting)                │
│                                                         │
│  Is the task KNOWLEDGE (factual information)?            │
│  ├─ YES → ICL (put facts in context)                     │
│  │        Fine-tuning is poor for knowledge injection    │
│  │        and risks erasing existing knowledge           │
│                                                         │
│  Is knowledge retention critical?                        │
│  ├─ YES → LoRA (minimizes forgetting)                    │
│  └─ NO, just maximize target task accuracy              │
│     → SFT (highest peak performance)                    │
│                                                         │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

The three key takeaways:
1. There is no single best method — it depends on the task type (skill vs. knowledge), data availability, and whether forgetting is a concern
2. LoRA is the best general-purpose choice when you have moderate data and care about retaining the model's general capabilities
3. The skill vs. knowledge distinction is critical — skills need fine-tuning (SFT/LoRA), knowledge needs ICL or RAG

ACT 3: The Connection (15–23 min)

"I found your work and it resonated with what I've been seeing"

The goal: Bridge from YOUR work to HERS, naturally. This is where the paper comes up — but not as "I studied your paper." As "I noticed something in my work, and then I found your paper and it explained it."

The natural bridge:

"Actually, one thing I noticed when I was doing the Whisper fine-tuning — I used LoRA, and it seemed to preserve the model's general capabilities. I didn't think much of it at the time, but I recently came across a paper you co-authored — the one comparing SFT, LoRA, and ICL on Gemma 3. And it was really validating to see that systematic study of what I'd been intuitively doing."

Then — and this is key — share ONE genuine observation, not a laundry list of questions:

"The thing that really struck me was the weight delta analysis — how LoRA's changes concentrate in the upper layers. That made me wonder whether that would hold for speech models too, since the lower layers in a speech model are doing something very different from a text model."

Why this works:
1. You're not claiming expertise — you're sharing a genuine curiosity
2. It connects your work (pasketti/Whisper) to her work (the paper)
3. It's a real question, not a rehearsed one
4. It shows you actually read and understood the paper, without performing that understanding

If she engages: Great — let the conversation go where she takes it. She might talk about speech models, or she might pivot to robotics, or she might share something about the paper that's not in it.

If she goes a different direction: That's fine. Follow her. The paper is a tool, not a requirement. If she'd rather talk about TensorFlow, or her career pivot, or robotics, or coaching — go there. The goal is connection, not coverage.

Other natural bridges that might come up:

If she talks about her career pivot (Solaris → ML):

"That actually really resonates with me. I trained an LLM and then deployed it as bare C code on a microcontroller — I feel like I'm going in the opposite direction you did. You went from systems to ML, and I'm trying to bring ML back to systems."

If she talks about robotics/RT-X:

"I've been getting interested in multimodal models — adding vision to small models, things like that. Do you see VLA models going through the same adaptation challenges you found in the paper?"

If she mentions coaching or mentorship:

"That's actually one of the reasons I was excited to meet you. I'm still very new at Google and I'm trying to figure out how to navigate the systems and ML space. I'd love to hear how you think about mentorship and career development."

ACT 4: The Ask (23–28 min)

"I'd like to stay in touch — here's how you can reach me"

The goal: Plant the seed for long-term connection WITHOUT being pushy. You're not asking for weekly meetings. You're asking for permission to reach out occasionally.

The ask should be low-pressure and specific:

"I know you're incredibly busy, so I don't want to be presumptuous about your time. But I'd really love to stay in touch — even if it's just an occasional email. I'm working on some projects in the LLM training and deployment space, and having someone with your systems background to bounce ideas off of occasionally would be really valuable."

Even better — give her a reason to WANT to hear from you:

"I'm actually working on something right now that I think you'd find interesting — I'm exploring how to add vision capabilities to small language models. When I have something concrete, could I send you a short write-up? Even if you don't have time to respond, I'd love to share it with you."

Why this works:
1. It's low-commitment ("even if you don't have time to respond")
2. It's specific ("a short write-up" not "let's schedule a meeting")
3. It gives her a reason to say yes (she might actually be curious)
4. It respects her time

If she offers more — take it gratefully but don't push:
- If she says "let's do a follow-up in a few weeks" → "That would be amazing, thank you."
- If she says "just email me anytime" → "I'd love that, thank you. I'll keep it to occasional, thoughtful things — not spam."
- If she says "come to my office hours" (if she has them) → "Absolutely, I'll be there."

The Close (28–30 min)

"Thank you, genuinely"

"Thank you so much for taking the time to meet with me, Sherry. I know how packed your schedule must be. This was really encouraging for me — I've been figuring out a lot of this on my own, and it means a lot to have someone with your perspective take an interest."

Keep it short, warm, and genuine. Don't add caveats or qualifiers. Just mean it.

What NOT To Do

❌ Don't start with the paper. "I read your SFT/LoRA/ICL paper" as an opener is like going on a first date and saying "I read your LinkedIn." It's too much, too fast.

❌ Don't list your projects as a portfolio. "I did smol-llama, then vicharak-llam, then pasketti, then sign-gen..." That's a resume, not a conversation.

❌ Don't ask rapid-fire questions. One good question is worth more than five rehearsed ones. Let the conversation breathe.

❌ Don't try to show you know everything. If Sherry mentions something you don't know, say "I don't know much about that — could you tell me more?" That's more impressive than pretending.

❌ Don't be afraid of silence. If there's a pause, let it breathe. Sherry might be thinking about something to share. Don't rush to fill every gap.

❌ Don't over-rehearse. This guide is a compass, not a script. If the conversation goes somewhere unexpected, follow it. The best conversations are the ones that go off-script.

❌ Don't ask for too much. "Can we meet monthly?" is too much for a first meeting. "Can I email you occasionally?" is reasonable.

❌ Don't forget — she's a coach. Sherry is literally a certified professional coach. She WANTS to help people. You're not imposing by being genuine about wanting mentorship. You're giving her the opportunity to do what she loves.

The cadence:

  • Don't email more than once every 2-4 weeks
  • Always have something concrete to share (not just "checking in")
  • Always make it easy to ignore (no guilt, no pressure)
  • When she does respond, respond thoughtfully but don't over-respond

The One-Sentence Summary

Michu's job in this meeting is not to prove she's smart — it's to prove she's worth mentoring: someone genuinely curious, doing interesting work, easy to talk to, and respectful of Sherry's time.
