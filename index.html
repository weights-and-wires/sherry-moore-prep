🎭 The 30-Minute Story: How Michu Should Navigate Her First Meeting with Sherry Moore

The Big Picture: What This Meeting Actually Is

This is NOT a technical discussion. This is a first date for a mentorship relationship.

Think of it like this: Sherry is someone who could change the trajectory of Michu's career. She's a Senior Staff SWE who co-built TensorFlow, pivoted from kernel engineering to ML, AND is a certified coach who literally runs mentorship programs. She's the ideal mentor.

But Sherry is extremely busy. You won't get weekly 1:1s. You might not even get a follow-up for weeks. So this 30 minutes has one job:

Make Sherry remember Michu as someone worth investing in — and give Sherry a reason to want to stay in touch.

The technical depth (the SFT/LoRA/ICL guide, the research dossier) is your ammunition for later — when you do get a follow-up, or when you email her a thoughtful question. Today is about being interesting, being genuine, and being easy to mentor.

The Mindset Shift

❌ Wrong frame: "I need to impress Sherry with how much I know about her paper"

✅ Right frame: "I need Sherry to walk away thinking 'this person is doing really cool things, reminds me of why I got into this field, and I'd want to help them'"

Senior people at Google have met hundreds of junior engineers who try to impress them with knowledge. What actually makes them remember someone is:

  1. Genuine enthusiasm — not performed, but real passion for the work
  2. Interesting projects — things they haven't seen before
  3. Self-awareness — knowing what you don't know, asking good questions
  4. A natural connection — shared values, shared interests, shared story

The Narrative Arc (Not a Script — a Story to Tell)

Think of the 30 minutes as having four acts. Each one flows naturally into the next. You're not checking boxes — you're telling the story of who you are and why you're here.

ACT 1: The Introduction (0–5 min)

"Who I am and how I got here"

The goal: Be a real person, not a resume. Give Sherry enough context to understand you, but keep it warm and personal.

How to open:
- Thank her for the time. She's busy. Acknowledge it genuinely.
- Quick intro: "I'm Michu, I joined Google about 5 months ago — I'm a noogler, still finding my feet."
- Where you came from: "I did my undergrad at IIT Madras — dual degree in Stats and Data Science. I've been obsessed with ML systems for a while now."

The personal hook — make it human:
- Share WHY you got into ML. Not "I'm interested in LLM pre-training" — that's a buzzword. Instead:
- "I got into this because I wanted to understand how these models actually work from the ground up — not just call an API. That's why I ended up training a small LLaMA from scratch, just to see if I could."
- Or: "I've always been drawn to the systems side of ML — how do you actually run these models efficiently? That led me down some weird paths, like trying to run a tiny language model on a microcontroller."

Keep it conversational, not a monologue. Let her react. If she asks a question, follow it. If she shares something about herself, listen and engage.

ACT 2: The Work (5–15 min)

"Here's what I do — and it's genuinely interesting"

The goal: Show, don't tell. Let your projects be the proof that you're someone worth mentoring. But frame them as things you were curious about, not achievements you're listing.

The natural way to bring up your projects:

smol-llama:

"One thing I did recently — I wanted to understand pre-training end to end, so I trained a 360M LLaMA from scratch. Nothing fancy, just a small model, but I did the whole thing: custom tokenizer, training loop, everything. Ran it on a single H100 for about $53. It was more about learning than the model itself."

Why this works: It's humble ("nothing fancy"), shows initiative ("I wanted to understand"), and is concrete ("$53 on one H100"). It invites Sherry to ask questions without feeling like she's being quizzed.

vicharak-llam (your secret weapon):

"The weirdest thing I did recently — I took a tiny LLM, like 213K parameters, trained it in JAX, and then exported it to run as C firmware on a microcontroller. The chip has 264KB of SRAM. I had to figure out how to fit weights into flash and run inference by reading them over a serial interface. It was a fun exercise in how far you can push inference to the edge."

Why this is your best conversation starter: Sherry spent 14 years doing low-level systems work (Solaris kernel, firmware, hardware bring-up) before pivoting to ML. A story about running an LLM on a microcontroller is EXACTLY the kind of systems-meets-ML project that would resonate with her. She might even get excited about it.

pasketti (speech/ASR):

"I also did some work on ASR — fine-tuning Whisper for children's speech. That was a DrivenData competition. I used LoRA for the fine-tuning, and I noticed something interesting — the model seemed to retain its ability to handle adult speech even after fine-tuning on kids' speech. I didn't fully understand why at the time, but it stuck with me."

Why this is a setup move: You're planting a seed here. "LoRA preserved the model's general ability" — this is literally what Sherry's paper is about. But you're not saying "I read your paper." You're saying "I noticed this thing in my own work." When the paper comes up later (Act 3), the connection will feel organic.

Important: Don't list all three back to back. Bring up one, let Sherry react, and let the conversation flow. If she's interested in vicharak-llam, go deep on that. If she asks about smol-llama, talk about that. Follow her lead.

ACT 3: The Connection (15–23 min)

"I found your work and it resonated with what I've been seeing"

The goal: Bridge from YOUR work to HERS, naturally. This is where the paper comes up — but not as "I studied your paper." As "I noticed something in my work, and then I found your paper and it explained it."

The natural bridge:

"Actually, one thing I noticed when I was doing the Whisper fine-tuning — I used LoRA, and it seemed to preserve the model's general capabilities. I didn't think much of it at the time, but I recently came across a paper you co-authored — the one comparing SFT, LoRA, and ICL on Gemma 3. And it was really validating to see that systematic study of what I'd been intuitively doing."

Then — and this is key — share ONE genuine observation, not a laundry list of questions:

"The thing that really struck me was the weight delta analysis — how LoRA's changes concentrate in the upper layers. That made me wonder whether that would hold for speech models too, since the lower layers in a speech model are doing something very different from a text model."

Why this works:
1. You're not claiming expertise — you're sharing a genuine curiosity
2. It connects your work (pasketti/Whisper) to her work (the paper)
3. It's a real question, not a rehearsed one
4. It shows you actually read and understood the paper, without performing that understanding

If she engages: Great — let the conversation go where she takes it. She might talk about speech models, or she might pivot to robotics, or she might share something about the paper that's not in it.

If she goes a different direction: That's fine. Follow her. The paper is a tool, not a requirement. If she'd rather talk about TensorFlow, or her career pivot, or robotics, or coaching — go there. The goal is connection, not coverage.

Other natural bridges that might come up:

If she talks about her career pivot (Solaris → ML):

"That actually really resonates with me. I trained an LLM and then deployed it as bare C code on a microcontroller — I feel like I'm going in the opposite direction you did. You went from systems to ML, and I'm trying to bring ML back to systems."

If she talks about robotics/RT-X:

"I've been getting interested in multimodal models — adding vision to small models, things like that. Do you see VLA models going through the same adaptation challenges you found in the paper?"

If she mentions coaching or mentorship:

"That's actually one of the reasons I was excited to meet you. I'm still very new at Google and I'm trying to figure out how to navigate the systems and ML space. I'd love to hear how you think about mentorship and career development."

ACT 4: The Ask (23–28 min)

"I'd like to stay in touch — here's how you can reach me"

The goal: Plant the seed for long-term connection WITHOUT being pushy. You're not asking for weekly meetings. You're asking for permission to reach out occasionally.

The ask should be low-pressure and specific:

"I know you're incredibly busy, so I don't want to be presumptuous about your time. But I'd really love to stay in touch — even if it's just an occasional email. I'm working on some projects in the LLM training and deployment space, and having someone with your systems background to bounce ideas off of occasionally would be really valuable."

Even better — give her a reason to WANT to hear from you:

"I'm actually working on something right now that I think you'd find interesting — I'm exploring how to add vision capabilities to small language models. When I have something concrete, could I send you a short write-up? Even if you don't have time to respond, I'd love to share it with you."

Why this works:
1. It's low-commitment ("even if you don't have time to respond")
2. It's specific ("a short write-up" not "let's schedule a meeting")
3. It gives her a reason to say yes (she might actually be curious)
4. It respects her time

If she offers more — take it gratefully but don't push:
- If she says "let's do a follow-up in a few weeks" → "That would be amazing, thank you."
- If she says "just email me anytime" → "I'd love that, thank you. I'll keep it to occasional, thoughtful things — not spam."
- If she says "come to my office hours" (if she has them) → "Absolutely, I'll be there."

The Close (28–30 min)

"Thank you, genuinely"

"Thank you so much for taking the time to meet with me, Sherry. I know how packed your schedule must be. This was really encouraging for me — I've been figuring out a lot of this on my own, and it means a lot to have someone with your perspective take an interest."

Keep it short, warm, and genuine. Don't add caveats or qualifiers. Just mean it.

What NOT To Do

❌ Don't start with the paper. "I read your SFT/LoRA/ICL paper" as an opener is like going on a first date and saying "I read your LinkedIn." It's too much, too fast.

❌ Don't list your projects as a portfolio. "I did smol-llama, then vicharak-llam, then pasketti, then sign-gen..." That's a resume, not a conversation.

❌ Don't ask rapid-fire questions. One good question is worth more than five rehearsed ones. Let the conversation breathe.

❌ Don't try to show you know everything. If Sherry mentions something you don't know, say "I don't know much about that — could you tell me more?" That's more impressive than pretending.

❌ Don't be afraid of silence. If there's a pause, let it breathe. Sherry might be thinking about something to share. Don't rush to fill every gap.

❌ Don't over-rehearse. This guide is a compass, not a script. If the conversation goes somewhere unexpected, follow it. The best conversations are the ones that go off-script.

❌ Don't ask for too much. "Can we meet monthly?" is too much for a first meeting. "Can I email you occasionally?" is reasonable.

❌ Don't forget — she's a coach. Sherry is literally a certified professional coach. She WANTS to help people. You're not imposing by being genuine about wanting mentorship. You're giving her the opportunity to do what she loves.

After the Meeting: The Follow-Up Strategy

Within 24 hours (Aug 13 evening):

Send a short, warm email:

Hi Sherry,

Thank you so much for taking the time to meet with me today. I really enjoyed our conversation — especially hearing about [specific thing she mentioned that you found genuinely interesting].

I'm working on [current project] and I'll share something concrete when I have it. No rush to respond — I just wanted to make sure you knew how much I appreciated your time.

Best,
Michu

In 2–3 weeks (when you have something concrete):

Send the thing you promised — a short write-up, a demo, a finding from your work:

Hi Sherry,

I mentioned I was working on [X]. Here's a short write-up of what I've been finding — [2-3 paragraphs, not a thesis]. I thought you might find it interesting given our conversation about [Y].

Would love your thoughts whenever you have a moment — no rush at all.

Best,
Michu

The cadence:

  • Don't email more than once every 2-4 weeks
  • Always have something concrete to share (not just "checking in")
  • Always make it easy to ignore (no guilt, no pressure)
  • When she does respond, respond thoughtfully but don't over-respond

The One-Sentence Summary

Michu's job in this meeting is not to prove she's smart — it's to prove she's worth mentoring: someone genuinely curious, doing interesting work, easy to talk to, and respectful of Sherry's time.

The technical prep (the deep-dive guide, the research dossier) matters — but not for this meeting. It matters for the follow-up, for the second conversation, for the moment when Sherry says "tell me more about that." That's when you deploy it.

Today, just be Michu — the noogler who trained an LLM from scratch, ran one on a microcontroller, fine-tuned Whisper for kids' speech, and is genuinely excited to learn from someone who's been doing systems-meets-ML since before Michu was born.
